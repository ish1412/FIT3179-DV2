NAPLAN 2008–2026

Same Test, Different Starting Lines

What NAPLAN 2023–2026 tells us about who gets left behind

Ishan · FIT3179 · Oct 2026

1 One in three

Every year, almost every Australian student in Years 3, 5, 7 and 9 sits NAPLAN, the National Assessment Program – Literacy and Numeracy. It tests five subjects (reading, writing, spelling, grammar and punctuation, and numeracy) and places each student at one of four proficiency levels.

The benchmark is Strong: meeting what is expected at that year level. In 2026, averaged across subjects and year levels, about one in three students (34%) fell short of it. In Year 5 it was 31%, roughly 3 children in every 10.

This page asks who those students are, where they live, and whether the gap closes as they move through school.

The four levels

  • Exceeding
    Well above what's expected at this year level.
  • Strong
    Meeting the expected standard: challenging but reasonable.
  • Developing
    Working towards the expected standard.
  • Needs additional support
    Not yet at the expected standard. Likely to need extra help.

"Strong or Exceeding" (ACARA calls it "Strong or above") is the headline measure of meeting expectations.

2 The flat line

On the old scale (2008–2022), scores crept up, mostly in the early years. Year 3 Reading rose 37 points and Year 5 Reading 25 points, while Year 9 barely moved.

There was no test in 2020 because of COVID-19. In 2023 NAPLAN moved fully online with a new scale and new proficiency levels, so the line breaks there: scores on either side of the break can't be compared.

Since the reset, results have hardly moved. Of the 20 subject and year-level combinations, 14 changed by less than 2 percentage points between 2023 and 2026. The biggest fall was Year 7 Grammar & Punctuation (−3.9 points) and the biggest rise was Year 5 Numeracy (+3.0).

Grammar & Punctuation is the weakest subject: in Year 3 and Year 9, about 44% of students sit below Strong.

3 Where you live

Where a child goes to school matters. The map below is a cartogram: each state is stretched or shrunk so its area matches how many students it has in a year level (k = thousand). Student numbers hardly change between subjects or year levels, so the map stays the same when you change the filters. Each state keeps the same colour as its line in the chart beside it. In 2026 (Year 5, average of subjects), Victoria and the ACT (73%) and NSW (71%) had the largest shares of students at Strong or Exceeding. The Northern Territory had 41%: 27 points below the national figure of 68%, and about 30 behind the leaders. ACARA did not publish Tasmania's 2026 results, so it is grey on the map.

Click a state to follow it in the line and ranking charts.

The order barely changes. Since 2023 the ACT and Victoria have swapped first and second place, while NSW has stayed third, WA fourth, SA fifth and Queensland sixth.

The Northern Territory has been last every year. It only rises to seventh in 2026 because Tasmania has no 2026 result. No state's share has moved by more than 2 points in four years.

4 The postcode effect

To see why place matters, look at schools rather than states. Every school has an ICSEA score (Index of Community Socio-Educational Advantage). It is built mostly from the education and jobs of students' parents, plus the school's remoteness and its share of Indigenous students.

1000 is the Australian average: above it means more advantaged, below it less. The map below shows all 9,619 schools, coloured by ICSEA. Drag across the histograms to show only the schools in that range, or zoom to a state.

Advantage thins out with distance. The typical major-city school has an ICSEA of 1049; in very remote areas it is 707.

28% of major-city schools are below the average of 1000, compared with 84% of remote schools and 97% of very remote ones.

Results follow the same path. Nationally (Year 5, average of subjects), 72% of major-city students reach Strong or Exceeding, falling to 55% in outer regional areas, 44% in remote areas and 24% in very remote ones.

The drop is steepest in the Northern Territory: 57% in the outer regional area that includes Darwin, 31% in remote areas and just 8% in very remote ones. Zoom into a state to compare its own zones.

5 Who's behind

Three gaps, side by side (Year 5, 2026):

Indigeneity. Indigenous students are behind everywhere, and the gap widens with distance: 24–30 points in cities and regional areas, 46 points in very remote areas, where only 8% of Indigenous students reach Strong. This overlaps with section 4: Indigenous students make up 4% of enrolments in major-city schools but 67% in very remote ones.

Parents' schooling. 81% of children whose parents hold a bachelor degree reach Strong, compared with 37% when parents left school in Year 11 or earlier.

Gender. The smallest gap, and it runs both ways. Girls lead in reading, writing, spelling and grammar (by 10 points in writing); boys lead in numeracy by 6.

6 Growing up with the gap

Does school close the gap? The chart follows students between tests, two years apart. Indigenous and non-Indigenous students gain almost the same number of points at every step (85 vs 83 from Year 3 to Year 5, average of subjects), so the gap they start with stays put: about 78 points in Year 3 and still about 78 in Year 9. In 2026 the average Indigenous Year 9 student scored about the same as the average non-Indigenous Year 5 student (494 vs 495).

Takeaways

  • About one in three students is below Strong, and that share has barely moved since 2023.
  • The biggest gaps come from circumstance, not gender: remoteness, Indigeneity and parents' schooling each mean gaps of roughly 30 to 50 points; gender means 3 to 10 (Year 5).
  • The gaps are already there in Year 3 and don't close by Year 9. Students who start behind make the same progress as everyone else, not more.
  • Closing the gap means helping those students gain more than their peers, and starting early.
